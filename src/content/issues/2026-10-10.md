Each post this week found a signal that said the right thing and meant the wrong one. A probe
returned in 15 ms while queued requests timed out. A priority field read HIGH on nearly every job.
An agent closed a ticket and the database disagreed. The fixes share a shape: stop trusting the
self-report and measure the state behind it.

---

## Zalando's circuit breaker stayed open for hours because its probes told the truth

Zalando traced a client-side circuit breaker that blocked roughly 95% of requests for hours while
the downstream service showed no errors and no restarts. A morning surge tripped the breaker to
half-open. Probe traffic fell to about 5 requests per second per pod, so the autoscaler shrank the
service to its minimum of three pods. Probes returned in 15 ms at the client and 2 to 3 ms on the
service, passed the success check, and the breaker closed. The client released its whole queue onto
three pods. Service time stayed at 3 ms, requests waited over 100 ms in queue, spans timed out, and
the breaker reopened within about three seconds. A three-second burst is invisible to CPU
autoscaling that averages over minutes.

The fix was to force-close the breaker and take a brief error spike while pods scaled, or to pre-
scale before closure. The proposals are the part to copy: autoscale on queue depth or concurrency,
lengthen the half-open window, shed load at the boundary, and propagate cancellations. The post
gives no breaker thresholds or customer impact.

## Ai2 replaces priority labels with GPU budgets and a 30-day scorecard

The Allen Institute for AI runs clusters of 88 to 1,024 GPUs for about 150 researchers, with demand
at two to three times supply. Priority scheduling had decayed: nearly every job claimed HIGH and
researchers parked no-op jobs to hold GPUs. The replacement gives each project a percentage of
cluster capacity, schedules by fair share over a seven-day lookback, and asks each workload to
declare a minimum runtime it is protected for. Over a 30-day test, teams received 98% of the GPU
hours owed, 13 of 15 allocations hit 95% or better, and the worst case was 90%. Occupancy held at
98%. The p90 debug queue wait fell from 2 hours to 30 seconds, median queue latency on the largest
cluster from 5 minutes to 24 seconds, and on-call repairs needing a human fell 74%.

The tradeoffs are named. Protection caps at eight hours, which broke multi-day interactive sessions
that now get preempted. The team is watching whether minimum runtimes fragment capacity and lengthen
waits for the largest jobs. Utilization, as opposed to occupancy, is still unmeasured.

## ThinkingBox grades agents on the database, not on what they said they did

Microsoft and Hugging Face built ThinkingBox from 507 stateful business tasks in five domains, each
run 20 times per model and graded by executable assertions on the final database state. Across
121,680 valid trials, 65.56% failed those checks, and 67.24% of the failures ended cleanly with
valid tool calls and no error reported. Claude Opus 5.5 led single-attempt accuracy at 67.16%, yet
it and Claude Opus 5 passed exactly the same 241 tasks on all 20 runs, so the newer model bought no
dependability. Kimi-K3 solved 93.89% of tasks at least once and 13.41% every time.

The cost framing is the useful part. GPT-5.6 Sol is cheapest per successful attempt at $0.127 and
costs $9.76 per task it passes 20 of 20, so a demo and a dependable workflow rank models
differently. The authors recommend human approval for irreversible changes and a smaller tool
surface, and say plainly they have not measured the lift from either.

## PlanetScale publishes TIN's search benchmark with its best-case conditions attached

TIN v1.0.6, PlanetScale's Postgres full-text search extension, adds stemming in 18 languages through
Snowball algorithms, which requires an index rebuild, and a block-max tiebreaker so a secondary
ORDER BY no longer costs performance. The benchmark is an 85 GB Stack Exchange corpus of 150 million
documents and 1,254 synthetic queries. On x86-64, conjunction queries ran at 842 queries per second
with a 57 ms p99 against ParadeDB's 169 and 253 ms; disjunctions 236 and 132 ms against 65 and 532
ms. TIN's index held at 52.1 GB while ParadeDB 0.26.0 grew to 67.3 GB.

Two choices deserve attention. By default TIN drops terms found in more than 10% of documents from
the BM25 score to save CPU; they stay searchable, and a full-score function restores them. And the
post states its caveats: unchanging tables, a single-shot index build, a clean VACUUM, and an index
about twice shared buffers. Concurrent-write performance is claimed but not re-measured, and the
AVX-512 dependence means the numbers will not transfer to every host.

[source →](https://planetscale.com/blog/tin-v106)

## Cloudflare profiles a live Worker by holding the isolate lock as briefly as it can

Cloudflare's on-demand profiling attaches to a Worker or Durable Object already running in
production and returns a flamegraph in the dashboard or CLI. The runtime locates the right version,
data center, isolate and account, then takes the isolate lock only to start V8's CPU profiler at a
one millisecond sampling interval, releases it, waits the requested duration, five seconds by
default, reacquires it to stop, and serializes outside the lock. The worked example is a JSON
replacer made 2.7x faster, and a memory fix that moved P50 heap from 70 MB to 54 MB and P999 from
133 MB to 118 MB against the 128 MB Worker limit.

The limits are stated. Sessions start by hand, so the misbehavior has to recur. The memory profiler
sees only allocations inside the window, not startup. A Worker needs enough traffic to be sampled.
The post does not give the profiler's own overhead, or any quota or pricing.

[source →](https://blog.cloudflare.com/workers-on-demand-profiling/)
